00 The problem, in one minute

This study concerns the DYAMOND simulation output currently held in temporary storage at /capstor/store1/cscs/userlab/cwp03/zemanc/Data_Dyamond*. The question we set out to answer is operational: where and how should this archive ultimately be stored — on capstor or on VAST, and compressed or uncompressed?

The archive holds 872,951 files totalling 6.61 PiB. Storing and moving data at this scale is expensive, so the underlying goal is concrete: make the archive substantially smaller without discarding anything a scientist would actually need. This report works through the evidence end to end — how the data was sampled, how a compression recipe was chosen and proven safe, how it behaves across resolutions, and how it compares with the simpler VAST route — so the storage decision can rest on measurements rather than intuition.

The archive spans three model resolutions — R02B06, R02B08, and R02B10 — and the finest, R02B10, dominates it almost entirely:

To shrink the data we re-pack it with a compression recipe. The hard part is not the squeezing — it is choosing a recipe that squeezes hard while provably preserving the physics. This report explains how we found such a recipe, how we proved it is safe, and what is left before the whole archive is compressed.

01 A glossary for newcomers

Five ideas are enough to follow everything that follows.

The five words that matter

NetCDF & Zarr — two file formats
NetCDF is the format the simulation wrote. Zarr is the modern format we convert to: it stores an array as many small "chunks," which makes it fast to read in parallel and friendly to compression. Think of it as moving from one giant document to a well-organised binder.
Compression ratio (CR)
How many times smaller the data becomes. CR = original size ÷ compressed size. A CR of 9 means the file is one-ninth its original size. Bigger is better.
Lossless vs. lossy
Lossless compression is perfectly reversible — like a ZIP file — but on this kind of scientific data it barely helps (about 1.5×). Lossy compression throws away detail too fine to matter (the digits past what the instrument could ever measure), which is what unlocks the big savings. The entire challenge is doing this safely.
A "codec" — the recipe — = three parts working together
The recipe applied to each variable is a codec stack: three layers, applied in order. We use the words "recipe" and "codec stack" interchangeably throughout.
• Serializer — how numbers are turned into bytes (the fundamental representation). Our two options are ZFPY, which is excellent on smooth fields, and PCodec, which tolerates noisy ones.
• Filter — an array-to-array transform that prepares the data for the compressor. A few filters are lossless (e.g. Delta), but the ones we mostly rely on — BitRound and Quantize — are deliberately lossy: they round away trailing precision that carries no real information. That rounding is exactly where this pipeline's loss comes from — which is why it is held inside a strict, gate-checked budget (§05). The compressor that follows is always lossless.
• Compressor — a general byte-squeezer applied last (e.g. LZMA, BZ2, Zstd), the same family as everyday ZIP tools.
Fidelity gates
A panel of automatic quality checks. A recipe is only allowed to win if every check passes — guaranteeing the compressed data stays within a tiny, pre-agreed error budget of the original. This is the scientific safety net, explained in §05.

Throughout, a "field" means one physical variable at one resolution — for example, temperature at the finest grid, or sea-level pressure at the coarsest. Each field gets its own tailored recipe.

02 The two-phase strategy

A recipe — the per-variable codec stack of serializer + filter + compressor from §01 — must be chosen for each kind of data. We could never test every candidate recipe on every one of the 872,951 files, so we split the work into a cheap thinking phase and an expensive doing phase.

Phase 1 — Discovery sweep cheap

Take one small representative sample of each field. Try thousands of recipes on it. Score each by how much it shrinks the data and whether it survives the fidelity gates. Record the single best recipe per field in a small file called a manifest. This runs in minutes per field and touches a tiny fraction of the data.

Phase 2 — Persist expensive

Take the winning recipe from each manifest and apply it to every matching file in the archive — hundreds of thousands of them. No searching here, just applying the already-chosen answer. This is the heavy, large-scale step that actually reclaims the storage.

This report is mostly about Phase 1 — finding and trusting the recipes — because that is where all the scientific judgement lives. Phase 2 is described at the end (§09). The key insight that makes the split worthwhile: the best recipe depends mostly on the variable, not on the individual file, so a recipe learned from one sample can be reused across thousands of files of the same variable.

03 Choosing 257 representative fields

We did not pick files at random. We picked exactly one per distinct kind of data.

Every file in the archive belongs to a combination of resolution × output-stream × variable (the "stream" is just which group of variables were written together, like out_11 for pressure-level fields). Files that share the same combination are statistically near-identical from a compression standpoint — the same variable on the same grid, only a different day. So one well-chosen file represents all its siblings.

That logic gives 257 representative fields: 120 at the coarsest resolution (R02B06) and 137 at the finest (R02B10). These 257 are the entire subject of the discovery sweep. The three resolutions differ enormously in size, which is exactly why the recipe must be checked at more than one of them (§07):

04 The sweep: 8,844 recipes per field

For each of the 257 fields, we built every recipe the toolkit can express and tested them all on the sample.

Each codec layer comes in a handful of families, and every family is instantiated at many parameter settings:

These families are not used as-is. Each is first instantiated into a list of concrete codec objects — one object per parameter setting: BitRound at each candidate keep-bits value, Quantize at each digit count, ZFPY at each accuracy / precision / rate setting, LZMA at each preset, Blosc at each (algorithm, level, byte-shuffle), and so on. The recipe space is then the Cartesian product of the three resulting lists — every instantiated serializer × every instantiated filter × every instantiated compressor (literally an itertools.product(compressors, filters, serializers)) — which for a typical field expands to 8,844 fully-specified recipes. Each recipe is applied to the field's sample, the result is decompressed, and we measure two things: how much smaller it got, and how far the decompressed values drifted from the original.

To keep this affordable we used carefully sized samples — a default budget of 2 GiB per field, dropped further for a handful of memory-heavy 3-D fields. We confirmed that the answer (which recipe wins) is already stable well below that budget, so smaller samples do not change the verdict. The sweep ran on the CSCS "Santis" supercomputer across eight nodes per job, packed into cost-aware batches by how long each field was expected to take.

Why the winner is trustworthy from a small sample

Compression behaviour is governed by a field's statistics — its smoothness, its range, how much genuine randomness it contains. Those statistics converge quickly: a few gigabytes of a field look, statistically, like the whole field. And we sample deliberately rather than grabbing one contiguous block: the toolkit keeps the full horizontal grid intact — so the codecs still see the real spatial structure they exploit — while thinning only the time and vertical axes, picking evenly-spaced slices through them with np.linspace (edge-inclusive and deterministic, so the same sample is reproduced when the recipe is later applied). Draining the time axis first captures temporal variety, the most informative axis for ranking codecs. The result spans the field's full range instead of one localised snapshot — exactly what a fair codec comparison needs. So the recipe that wins on the sample is overwhelmingly the recipe that would win on the full data. The persist phase later re-checks this on real files (§09), so nothing rides on the assumption alone.

05 The fidelity gates — our safety net

This is the heart of the method. A recipe that shrinks the data 300× is worthless if it corrupts the science. The gates decide what "corrupt" means — and we chose them deliberately.

Among the thousands of recipes, the high-compression ones are always lossy: they discard detail. The question is which detail is safe to discard. We answer it by forcing every candidate recipe through a panel of eight independent error checks. A recipe is "kept" only if it passes all eight; the winner is the highest-compression recipe still standing.

How each gate is computed

Let x be the original values and y the values recovered after compression, with every sum taken over the cells that are finite in the original. Each metric is a relative error — normalised by the field's own magnitude — so one "1%" budget means the same thing across variables with wildly different units and scales.

Here τ is the field's L1 budget (the master dial, ≤ 1%) and Q99 is the 99th percentile of |x|, so the q99 gate scores error only on the most extreme 1% of cells. The bias denominator is identical to L1's, so bias ≤ L1 always holds by construction. The gradient and bounds gates are activated only for the field types that need them (below).

How we actually chose the gating

The gate budgets were not picked by taste. Two principles drove them:

1. The budget starts from the field's own variability, following the climate-compression literature. The guiding principle, from Klöwer et al. (2021) and Baker et al. (2014; 2016/2017), is that the error introduced by compression must stay well below a field's natural variability — only the bits carrying real information should be kept. We make that concrete by requiring the absolute mean (L1) error to remain below 0.5% of the field's standard deviation σ: mean|y − x| < 0.005·σ. The error is then an order of magnitude or more below the signal's own spread, so it cannot masquerade as physics.

The gates, however, are evaluated as relative errors (the table above divides by the field's own magnitude). So this absolute target is converted into each field's relative L1 budget by dividing by the field's mean magnitude: τ = 0.005·σ / mean|x|. That single step is why the budget adapts per field — temperature, whose spread is small next to its mean, lands at τ ≈ 0.055%; geopotential at τ ≈ 0.34%; while spiky fields such as precipitation and CAPE (large σ relative to a near-zero mean) would push τ higher. Two clamps finish the job: τ is capped at 1% so no field is ever allowed more than 1% relative mean error however large its variability, and floored at a small value for near-constant fields so we do not demand impossible precision. The rare field with no usable statistics falls back to a safe default.

Two different halves appear in this method and should not be confused: the 0.5% of σ above is the absolute design target that sets each field's budget; the ½τ bias limit in the gate panel is a separate rule capping one-directional drift at half of that budget.

2. Physics-driven extra gates, switched on by variable type. Not every gate applies to every field, and turning irrelevant gates on would needlessly reject good recipes. So the toolkit switches gates on by physical archetype:

Gradient gate → dynamical fields

Winds (u, v, w, omega), vertical velocity, pv, tke, temperature and geopotential. These carry meaning in their spatial texture — fronts, eddies, sharp transitions — so we explicitly forbid recipes that smear that structure, even slightly.

Extremes-sensitive → tail-driven fields

Precipitation, hail, hydrometeors (qr, qg, qc, qs, qi), and convection indices (CAPE, CIN). For these the rare extreme is the whole point, so the tail (q99) and worst-point gates are enforced more strictly.

Physical-bounds gates were likewise attached only to variables that have hard limits (non-negative quantities like humidity; the 0–100% range for cloud cover and relative humidity). This per-variable gate policy is what lets the sweep be both aggressive on smooth fields and conservative on dangerous ones, automatically.

References. The "error below natural variability" and "real information content" principles behind the threshold above come from M. Klöwer, M. Razinger, J. J. Domínguez, P. D. Düben & T. N. Palmer, Compressing atmospheric data into its real information content, Nature Computational Science 1, 713–724 (2021); and A. H. Baker et al., A methodology for evaluating the impact of data compression on climate simulation data (HPDC '14, 2014) and subsequent work (2016/2017) establishing that compression error should be statistically indistinguishable from a model's internal variability.

Lossy always wins — and it is not close

What the winning recipes look like

A clear pattern emerges across the 257 winners. One serializer dominates, and the compressor is almost always one of the two slow-but-thorough entropy coders that work best after a lossy filter has already collapsed the data's complexity:

In plain terms: ZFPY turns the smooth physical fields into compact bytes; a rounding filter (BitRound for clean physical fields, Quantize for accumulated or thresholded ones) discards the meaningless trailing precision; and a heavy compressor (LZMA or BZ2) squeezes what remains. This three-step shape recurs in almost every winner.

07 Does the recipe travel across resolutions?

We only swept two of the three resolutions. The whole plan depends on a recipe learned at one resolution working at another. So we tested that assumption hard — and the answer is nuanced but reassuring.

We compared the winning recipe for every variable present at both the coarse (R02B06) and fine (R02B10) resolutions — 106 comparable pairs. Asking whether the entire three-part recipe matches exactly is too strict a test, so we looked at which part disagrees, because the parts matter very unequally:

OutcomeCountHow much it matters
Exact recipe match53 (50%)Transfers perfectly.
Same serializer, different compressor/filter38Near-ties. The compressor choice (LZMA vs BZ2) is two flavours of the same idea working on already-prepared data; forcing the "wrong" one costs almost nothing.
Serializer flip (ZFPY ↔ PCodec)15 (14%)The only consequential disagreements. The serializer is the fundamental numeric representation; a flip here can genuinely cost compression.

Read the right way, this is good news: the serializer — the part that actually matters — agrees in 86% of cases (91 of 106). And the 15 flips are not random. They cluster in exactly one family of fields: the turbulent, convective, and moisture fields — vertical velocity, turbulent energy, raw hydrometeors, omega, potential vorticity, cloud cover. These are the high-randomness fields where ZFPY's smooth-field assumption and PCodec's noise-tolerance genuinely trade places. The smooth, well-behaved fields (pressure, temperature, fluxes, soil, snow) never flip.

Two things fall out of this. First, for every well-behaved field, the intermediate resolution lands on the same serializer as its neighbours — a clean third data point confirming the recipe transfers. Second, and just as informative: the handful of fields that refused to produce a winner at R02B08 are almost precisely the same turbulent/moisture fields that flip between the coarse and fine resolutions. The same intrinsic property — high, scale-dependent randomness — makes these fields both hard to gate and unstable across resolutions.

The cross-resolution verdict, in one paragraph

For the smooth, well-behaved majority — pressure, temperature, radiative and surface fluxes, soil and snow, winds on pressure levels — a recipe learned once transfers across all three resolutions (the compressor/filter may differ harmlessly). For the turbulent minority — raw moisture and hydrometeors, vertical velocity, convection indices — the serializer genuinely depends on resolution, so each is swept afresh at its own resolution rather than inheriting a neighbour's. And the maximally safe option is always on the table: skip the transfer assumption entirely and run the full discovery sweep on every field at every resolution. That simply costs more sweeps — still only on samples, never the full archive — so cross-resolution transfer is best seen as the optimisation that buys back that cost wherever the data proves it is safe.

08 The VAST alternative, compared

There is a second, completely different way to shrink the archive — and we benchmarked it too. It is worth understanding because the two approaches trade off in opposite directions.

The VAST approach converts each NetCDF file to Zarr with no compression at all, deliberately keeping the original chunk layout, and then relies on the storage hardware's built-in deduplication to reclaim space behind the scenes. (VAST is a storage appliance that automatically detects and stores identical data blocks only once.) The recipe-based sweep approach, by contrast, does the shrinking in the data itself with lossy codecs.

Sweep + codecs this report

Lossy, codec-based. Median ~9× smaller. Requires the per-variable sweep, the gate panel, and per-file fidelity verification. The savings are mathematical and portable — they live in the files and survive any copy or move.

VAST in-place + dedup benchmarked

Lossless, dedup-based. The conversion itself is byte-neutral — uncompressed Zarr is about the same size as the source NetCDF — so there is no inflation to undo. VAST's storage layer then deduplicates and compresses what was written: on the converted R02B06 trees it took 37.5 TiB logical down to 10.8 TiB physical, a measured data-reduction ratio of 3.5× — entirely lossless, with no per-variable tuning, and very fast.

VAST reports this directly on its dashboard: across the converted R02B06 trees the data-reduction ratio is 3.5×, entirely lossless. It was measured on the ~37.5 TiB converted so far, so the whole-archive figure may land higher or lower.

The VAST conversion is also fast and reliable: one production run converted 183,670 files in about 8.8 hours with zero failures or verification failures. Its parallelism is not MPI — just a single-node process pool. A worker is a separate process (its own memory) that handles whole files; threads run inside a worker (shared memory) to speed up a single file. More workers means more files in flight; more threads means each file finishes sooner. The sweet spot was 32 workers × 4 threads, sustaining ~531 MB/s:

They are not mutually exclusive — one could run codecs first and let dedup mop up afterwards, though the gains are sub-additive (lossy-compressed chunks are less likely to be byte-identical, so they dedupe less). The real trade-off: the sweep reaches ~9× but is lossy (within a bounded ≤1% error) and needs per-variable tuning, and its savings travel with the files; VAST reaches a measured ~3.5× losslessly, with no tuning and at high speed — but only while the data lives on the VAST appliance. If the archive will sit on VAST anyway and losslessness is paramount, ~3.5× essentially for free is hard to beat; if maximum, portable reduction is the goal and a small, gate-verified loss is acceptable, the sweep's ~9× wins.

09 From recipe to finished archive

With recipes chosen and trusted, Phase 2 applies them to all 872,951 files. Here is the full journey of a single file, end to end.

  1. Look up the recipe.
    The file's resolution, stream, and variable identify which manifest applies. The winning serializer + filter + compressor are read from it. No searching — the answer is already known.
  2. Read & re-pack into Zarr.
    The NetCDF file is read and written out as a compressed Zarr store using the recipe, with chunking aligned so the conversion is efficient.
  3. Verify the fidelity (the verify-gate).
    The freshly compressed data is decompressed and re-measured against the same eight gates the sweep used. If the real file somehow violates the error budget, the toolkit aborts that file rather than ship corrupted data. This turns the sweep's sample-based promise into a per-file guarantee, and it is on by default.
  4. Check the compression actually held (CR-drift).
    The achieved compression is compared to what the sweep predicted. A large drift (default ±25%) flags the file for review — a cheap early-warning that a file is unlike its representative sample.
  5. Consolidate & finalise.
    Metadata is consolidated so the store reads back quickly, and the result is recorded in an audit trail. The original is only ever removed after a successful verified write.

Because the verify-gate re-runs the full quality panel on real data, the large-scale phase is self-checking: a recipe that was safe on the sample but somehow unsafe on a particular file cannot slip through silently.

What full compression could reclaim — an order-of-magnitude estimate

The archive is 6.61 PiB, and 87% of it is the finest resolution, where the median shrink is about 10×. If the per-variable medians carry across the bulk of the data, the compressed archive would land in the neighbourhood of well under 1 PiB — a multi-petabyte saving. This is a projection, not a promise: the true archive-wide ratio depends on the exact mix of variables and on the handful of hard fields still being finalised. The verify-gate ensures that whatever ratio we get, the science inside it is intact.

10 The bottom line

The storage question comes down to a genuine trade-off rather than a single verdict. The codec sweep reaches roughly 9×, but lossily — within a strict, gate-checked ≤1% budget — and with per-variable tuning; its savings are portable and live in the files. VAST reaches a measured 3.5× data-reduction ratio losslessly, with no tuning and at high speed, but only while the data sits on the VAST appliance. If the archive will live on VAST and losslessness is the priority, 3.5× essentially for free is compelling; if maximum, portable reduction matters most and a bounded sub-1% error is acceptable, the ~9× sweep wins.

We learned the best compression recipe for each kind of data by trying nearly nine thousand recipes on small samples and keeping only those that pass an eight-point fidelity panel tuned to each variable's physics. We found that lossy compression beats per-file lossless by a wide margin and is safe under those gates; that the chosen serializer — the part that matters — transfers across resolutions for the smooth majority of fields while the turbulent minority needs its own sweep; and that the storage-dedup alternative (VAST) is faster and lossless and reaches a measured ~3.5× — substantial, though below the sweep's ~9×, and tied to the VAST hardware. What remains is the heavy, mechanical, self-checking step of applying the finished recipes to the whole archive.

One scoping choice is worth stating plainly. Every recipe here is built only from codecs available in the NumCodecs / Zarr ecosystem — a deliberately conservative, tried-and-tested foundation: well-maintained, widely deployed, and trivially readable by any standard Zarr client, with no exotic dependencies. The ~9× reported here is therefore a floor, not a ceiling. There is likely another tier of compression to unlock by bringing in codecs outside that core set — for example EBCC or NumCodecs-WASM — but as a first, defensible analysis we chose the bulletproof path and left those gains as a clearly-marked next step.
